\section{Results}
\subsection{Environmental Impact}
The environmental impact of the plant is assessed based on the following indicators

\begin{itemize}
    \item GHG emissions
    \item Water usage
    \item Land use
\end{itemize}

The assessment is conducted on the optimized operational scenario in which, for the first 20 years, both reactors are dedicated exclusively to electricity production and sale. After year 20, the secondary section of the plant—comprising High Temperature Steam Electrolyzer (HTSE) units and a methanation system coupled with a turbine—is introduced.

As a result, a higher impact on GHG emissions is expected in the early years, primarily due to the exclusive focus on electricity generation.

\subsubsection{GHG Emissions assessment}

\paragraph{Assumptions and Methodology}
GHG emissions are evaluated considering:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The impact on the regional energy mix (Lombardy), which is expected to be minor due to the localized nature of the plant.
    \item The impact on the local mix within the "Regione dei 7 Laghi", where the plant has a significantly more noticeable influence.
    \item Emissions related to residual fossil fuel demand for building heating.
    \item Emissions from the transportation sector, expected to decrease due to the hydrogen supplied by the plant directly to this sector.
\end{itemize}

For each of these, it is assumed that for the first 20 years the plant 
operates solely as an electricity provider. Starting from year 20, 
the hydrogen and methane production facilities come online, affecting 
the transportation and heating sectors respectively.

\paragraph{GHG emissions Data}
The data for the current (2023) Lombardy energy mix were sourced from Terna, including imported energy shares. Greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions per energy source are based on median values reported in Daniel Weisser's (2007) article: “A guide to life-cycle greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from electric supply technologies.” ~\cite{weisser} Specifically, solar photovoltaic (PV) was considered for the "Renewable" share, as it is the most prevalent renewable source and the one of major interest for this study.

The values used are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coal}: 996 gCO2eq/kWh
    \item \textbf{Natural Gas}: 550 gCO2eq/kWh
    \item \textbf{Oil}: 780 gCO2eq/kWh
    \item \textbf{Nuclear}: 12 gCO2eq/kWh
    \item \textbf{Renewables}: 56 gCO2eq/kWh
\end{itemize}

For nuclear power, other sources support values around 12 $gCO_{2}eq/kWh$, including: “Life cycle analysis of a network of small modular reactors” by Carlo L. Vinoya ~\cite{Vinoya}and “A comparative life-cycle assessment of hydro, nuclear and wind power: A China study” by Wang et al.~\cite{Wang}.

Equivalent GHG emissions for plant products are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hydrogen}: $19.39 gCO_{2}eq/kWh$, assuming electricity for HTSE is fully supplied by the two SMRs and using its LHV for evaluating the energy equivalent of hydrogen produced.
    \item \textbf{Syngas}: $72.76 gCO_{2}eq/kWh$, assuming all the hydrogen not stored for selling (produced with nuclear-provided electricity) is converted to syngas.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Energy consumption Data}
The baseline energy data prior to year 0 are derived from current statistics, with total electricity consumption and imports for Lombardy taken from Terna. Values are computed every 15 years to reflect assumed changes in building energy classifications and related energy demands. An exception is made for year 20 to account for the plant's secondary section's commissioning.

A key assumption is that the electricity produced by the proposed plant displaces imported electricity—an economically rational choice. The renewable share varies across scenarios, reflecting the increasing photovoltaic (PV) penetration over time.

\paragraph{Regional Energy Mix}
The regional impact is assessed in terms of how the plant alters Lombardy's energy mix, which is expected to be minimal due to its limited size (maximum output of 3 TWh/year vs. 65 TWh/year of regional consumption). Nevertheless, the impact is more significant in the first 20 years when the plant contributes only electricity.

The regional mix greenhouse gas (GHG) intensity is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
    \text{gCO2eq/kWh}_{mix} = \sum_{i} \left( \text{gCO2eq/kWh}_{i} \cdot \text{share}_{i} \right)
\end{equation}

where $i$ indexes each energy source and each energy source's share is taken into account.


\paragraph{Local Energy Mix}
Using a methodology analogous to the one applied at the regional level, the electricity consumption of the Seven Lakes Region is estimated based on monthly energy balances and photovoltaic production data gathered during this study. The local energy mix is reconstructed using three main components: Nuclear, Renewables, and Imported electricity. The evolution of this mix over time, and its impact on greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, is evaluated using the following approach:

\begin{equation}
    \text{gCO2eq/kWh}_{local-mix} = \sum_{i} \left( \text{gCO2eq/kWh}_{i} \cdot \text{share}_{i} \right)
\end{equation}

where $i$ indexes all considered energy sources.

For the imported share, the same $gCO_{2}eq/kWh$ intensity as the Lombardy regional mix is assumed.

\paragraph{Residual Fossil Fuel Consumption}
The greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions associated with the residual fossil fuel demand for residential heating are estimated using building demand data processed earlier in the study, alongside the amount of syngas produced and not consumed by the turbine.

The $gCO_{2}eq/kWh$ intensity for this sector is calculated by weighting:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the share of demand covered by syngas ($72.76 gCO_{2}eq/kWh$),
    \item the share covered by natural gas ($550 gCO_{2}eq/kWh$).
\end{itemize}

This impact is zero in the first 20 years of operation, as the plant only provides electricity. After year 20, the introduction of syngas production leads to a growing influence on this sector.

While fossil fuel demand is projected to decrease over time due to improved building energy performance and increased photovoltaic (PV) penetration, syngas production remains constant—resulting in a gradually increasing relative contribution to GHG mitigation.

\subsubsection{Water Usage}
The impact on water resources, primarily required for reactor and Balance of Plant (BoP) operation as well as for the High Temperature Steam Electrolyzer (HTSE) units, is deemed negligible. This is due to the plant's location near Lake Maggiore—one of Italy's largest lakes—which ensures a stable water supply without significant disruption to the basin's hydrological balance or ecosystem.

\subsubsection{Land Use}
One of the key advantages of the proposed project is its minimal land use impact. The compact scale of the facility, combined with its placement in an area already approved for nuclear activities, ensures efficient use of space. The site is located within the Joint Research Centre (JRC) complex, which already hosts research reactors (ESSOR and Ispra-1) and associated nuclear infrastructure. Notably, a containment building is available on-site and is deemed suitable to house at least one of the two planned Small Modular Reactors (SMRs), greatly reducing the need for new construction and environmental disturbance.

\section{Possible Improvements}
\subsection{On the Data Collection}
It is definitely possible and could yield better results if the data for each class were obtained from a set of real buildings in the region instead of just one. Ideally, one would use a statistically significant set of buildings and vary the utilization profile as well. Moreover, it would be beneficial to weight the simulation data on the real energy requirements of those same buildings. This is a much more complex task since it would require extensive data acquisition throughout the year.

While this is feasible for electrical demand (as most operators provide customers with a detailed bill showing hourly consumption), it is much more difficult to obtain the same data for fossil fuel consumption. Additionally, the normalization has been done with the heated area and not with the volume (which is much more indicative of the energy requirement of a building). This is based on the on-field knowledge that the volume is usually approximately obtained by multiplying the heated area by a factor of 2.7 or 3 in most cases.

From the simulation, we have neglected humidification and dehumidification electricity needs since these plants are rarely installed today. A better evaluation would consider the possible future share of air treatment plants in the residential sector. The share of installed air conditioning systems for cooling might increase in the future but for the purpose of this study it was assumed constant over the years.

\paragraph{Renewable Energy Sources}
We have neglected any solar-thermal systems installed since they are considered not very impactful and their impact is complex to evaluate.

\paragraph{Conversion to Primary Energy}
Using time-dependent efficiency for heat pumps that take into account the ambient temperature could improve the model. This could be accomplished considering that our code uses hourly data.

\paragraph{Other Electricity Demand}
This is without a doubt the most complex contribution to evaluate, especially considering that it is not easy to imagine how electricity consumption will evolve in 25 years. It could be improved by collecting a statistically significant number of examples from the region on the type of appliances installed and their consumption.

The methodology of using class distribution as a way of evaluating energy demand is considered a good approach. For the best results, we should gather consumption data for both electric and fossil fuels for a statistically significant number of buildings for each energy class.
