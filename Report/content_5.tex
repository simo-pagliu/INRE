\section{Results}
\subsection{Environmental Impact}
The environmental impact of the plant is assessed based on the following 
indicators:

\begin{itemize}
    \item GHG emissions
    \item H$_2$O usage
    \item Land use
\end{itemize}

The assessment is conducted on the optimized operational scenario in 
which, for the first 20 years, both reactors are dedicated exclusively 
to electricity production and sale. After year 20, the secondary section 
of the plant—comprising HTSE units and a methanation system coupled with 
a turbine—is introduced.

As a result, a higher impact on GHG emissions is expected in the early 
years, primarily due to the exclusive focus on electricity generation.

\subsubsection{GHG Emissions assessment}

\paragraph{Assumptions and Methodology}
GHG emissions are evaluated considering:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The impact on the regional energy mix (Lombardy), which is 
    expected to be minor due to the localized nature of the plant.
    \item The impact on the local mix within the "Regione dei 7 Laghi," 
    where the plant has a significantly more noticeable influence.
    \item Emissions related to residual fossil fuel demand for building 
    heating.
    \item Emissions from the transportation sector, expected to decrease 
    due to the hydrogen supplied by the plant directly to this sector.
\end{itemize}

For each of these, it is assumed that for the first 20 years the plant 
operates solely as an electricity provider. Starting from year 20, 
the hydrogen and methane production facilities come online, affecting 
the transportation and heating sectors respectively.

\paragraph{GHG emissions Data}
The data for the current (2023) Lombardy energy mix were sourced from 
Terna, including imported energy shares.

GHG emissions per energy source are based on median values reported 
in Daniel Weisser's (2007) article: “A guide to life-cycle greenhouse 
gas (GHG) emissions from electric supply technologies.” ~\cite{weisser}

Specifically, SOlar PV was considered for the "Renewable" share, 
as it is the most prevalent renewable source and the one of major 
interest for this study.

The values used are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coal}: 996 gCO2eq/kWh
    \item \textbf{Natural Gas}: 550 gCO2eq/kWh
    \item \textbf{Oil}: 780 gCO2eq/kWh
    \item \textbf{Nuclear}: 12 gCO2eq/kWh
    \item \textbf{Renewables}: 56 gCO2eq/kWh
\end{itemize}

For nuclear power, other sources support values around 12 gCO$_2$eq/kWh, 
including:
*“Life cycle analysis of a network of small modular reactors”* by 
Carlo L. Vinoya ~\cite{Vinoya}and *“A comparative life-cycle assessment 
of hydro-, nuclear and wind power: A China study”* by Wang et al.
~\cite{Wang}.

Equivalent GHG emissions for plant products (hydrogen and syngas) are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hydrogen}: 19.39 gCO$_2$eq/kWh, assuming electricity 
    for HTSE is fully supplied by the two SMRs and using its LHV for
    evaluating the energy equivalent of H_$2$ produced.
    \item \textbf{Syngas}: 72.76 gCO$_2$eq/kWh, assuming all the hydrogen 
    not stored for selling (produced with nuclear-provided electricity)
    is converted to syngas.
\end{itemize}


\paragraph{Energy consumption Data}
The baseline energy data prior to year 0 are derived from current 
statistics, with total electricity consumption and imports for Lombardy 
taken from Terna.

Values are computed every 15 years to reflect assumed changes in building energy classifications 
and related energy demands. An exception is made for year 20 to account 
for the plant's secondary section's commissioning.

A key assumption is that the electricity produced by the proposed plant 
displaces imported electricity—an economically rational choice.

The renewable share varies across scenarios, reflecting the increasing PV 
penetration over time.

\paragraph{Regional Energy Mix}
The regional impact is assessed in terms of how the plant alters 
Lombardy's energy mix, which is expected to be minimal due to its 
limited size (max output of 3 TWh/year vs. 65 TWh/year of regional 
consumption).

Nevertheless, the impact is more significant in the first 20 years 
when the plant contributes with only electricity.

The regional mix GHG intensity is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
    \text{gCO2eq/kWh}_{mix} = \sum_{i} \left( \text{gCO2eq/kWh}_{i} \cdot \text{share}_{i} \right)
\end{equation}

where $i$ indexes each energy source and each enegy source's share is
yaken into account.


\paragraph{Local Energy Mix}
Using a methodology analogous to the one applied at the regional level, 
the electricity consumption of the 7 Lakes Region is estimated based
on monthly energy balances and photovoltaic production data gathered 
during this study. The local energy mix is reconstructed using three 
main components: \textit{Nuclear}, \textit{Renewables}, 
and \textit{Imported} electricity.
The evolution of this mix over time, and its impact on GHG emissions, 
is evaluated using the approach as for the regional 
mix:

\begin{equation}
    \text{gCO2eq/kWh}_{local-mix} = \sum_{i} \left( \text{gCO2eq/kWh}_{i} \cdot \text{share}_{i} \right)
\end{equation}

where $i$ indexes all considered energy sources.

For the imported share, the same gCO$_2$eq/kWh intensity as the Lombardy 
regional mix is assumed.

\paragraph{Residual Fossil Fuel Consumption}
The GHG emissions associated with the residual fossil fuel demand for 
residential heating are estimated using building demand data processed 
earlier in the study, alongside the amount of syngas produced and not 
consumed by the turbine.

The gCO$_2$eq/kWh intensity for this sector is calculated by weighting:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the share of demand covered by syngas (72.76 gCO$_2$eq/kWh),
    \item the share covered by natural gas (550 gCO$_2$eq/kWh).
\end{itemize}

Naturally, this impact is zero in the first 20 years of operation, 
as the plant only provides electricity. After year 20, the introduction 
of syngas production leads to a growing influence on this sector.

It is important to note that while fossil fuel demand is projected to 
decrease over time (due to improved building energy performance and 
increased PV penetration), syngas production remains constant—resulting 
in a gradually increasing relative contribution to GHG mitigation.


\paragraph{Transportation Sector}
Transport energy consumption data for the region were estimated by 
scaling national figures according to the population share of the 7 
Lakes Region. The national data were retrieved from ~\cite{transportation}.

GHG intensities for diesel and gasoline (currently dominant in ground 
transport) were sourced from ~\cite{transportation_emissions}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Diesel: 266.5 gCO$_2$eq/kWh
    \item Gasoline: 263 gCO$_2$eq/kWh
\end{itemize}

The impact of the hydrogen produced by the plant is assessed by assuming 
that it is sold directly to the transportation sector. 
The resulting GHG intensity is calculated based on the share of 
energy demand met by:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Hydrogen (19.39 gCO$_2$eq/kWh), and
    \item Fossil fuels (264.75 gCO$_2$eq/kWh, average value between 
    diesel and gasoline).
\end{itemize}

Beyond the GHG reduction, it is worth highlighting that hydrogen 
substitution would also positively affect local air quality, 
due to the elimination of particulate matter emissions typically 
associated with diesel and gasoline combustion. However, this benefit 
is not quantified within this study.

\subsubsection{H_$2$O Usage}
The impact on water resources, mainly required for reactor and BoP 
operation as well as for the HTSE units, is deemed negligible. 
This is due to the plant's location near Lake Maggiore—one of Italy's 
largest lakes—which ensures a stable water supply without significant 
disruption to the basin's hydrological balance or ecosystem.


\subsubsection{Land Use}
One of the key advantages of the proposed project is its minimal land use 
impact. The compact scale of the facility, combined with its placement 
in an area already approved for nuclear activities, ensures efficient 
use of space.
he site is located within the Joint Research Centre (JRC) complex, 
which already hosts research reactors (ESSOR and Ispra-1) and associated 
nuclear infrastructure. Notably, a containment building is available 
on-site, and is deemed suitable to house at least one of the two 
planned SMRs—greatly reducing the need for new construction and 
environmental disturbance.

\section{Possible Improvements}
\subsection{On the Data Collection}
It is defenitly possible and could give better results if the data for each class was obtained by a set of real building from the region instead of just one.
Ideally one would use a statistically signfinicant set of buldings and vary the utilization profile as well.
Moreover, it would be beneficial to wheight the simulation data on the real energy requirments of those same buildings,
which is a much more complex task since it would require extensive data acquisition during the year. 
While this is feasible for electrical demand (most operators give to the customer a detailed bill with hourly consumption),
it is much more difficult to get the same data for fossil fuel consumption.
At last, the normalization has been done with the heated area an not with the volume (which is much more indicative of the energy requirment of a building) 
given the on-field knowledge that the volume is usually approximatly obtained by a the rule of thumb of multiplying the heated area by a factor of 2.7 or 3 in most cases.
From the simulation we have neglected humidification and dehumidification electricity needs since these plants are rearly installed today,
a better evaluation would evaluate the possible future share of air treatment plants in the residential sector.

Photovoltaic: We have neglected any solar-thermal system installed since their are considered not very impactful and their impact is complex to evaluate.

Convertion thermal to primary: use time dependent efficiency for heat pumps that take into account the ambient temperature,
this could be accomplished considering that our code uses hourly data.

Other Electricity Demand: This is without a doubt the most complex contribution to evaluate,
expecially considering that it is not easy to imagine how the electricity consumption will evolve in 25 years.
It could be improved by collecting a statistically signficant number of examples from the region on the type of appliances installed and their consumption.

Overall: the methodology of using class distribution as a way of evaluating the energy demand is considered a good approach,
for the best results we shall gather consumption data for both electric and fossil fuels for a statistically significant number of buildings for each energy class.
